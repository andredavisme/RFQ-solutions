# 06 — Westbrook Data Center Informed: Making Decisions With Complete Information, Negotiating Terms, and the Data Behind High-Stakes Agreements

**Source repo:** [andredavisme/westbrook-datacenter-informed](https://github.com/andredavisme/westbrook-datacenter-informed)  
**Completed:** 2026-06-10  
**Theme:** A civic information platform built to help a community make a consequential, time-bounded decision about a major external party (a data center developer) by understanding the full picture: what is being offered, what the real risks are, what alternatives exist, and how to negotiate terms that serve the community's interests. Every principle here applies to any business evaluating a vendor, customer, or partner relationship under time pressure.

---

## 1. The Moratorium as a Decision Discipline

On May 18, 2026, the Westbrook City Council adopted a 180-day moratorium on data center development. The ordinance cites eight specific findings of fact: disproportionate water and electricity consumption, grid strain, noise and heat impacts, inadequacy of existing land use ordinances, and the need for at least 180 days to gather information and develop regulations.

The moratorium is not a rejection. It is a structured pause to ensure that a decision of this magnitude is made with complete information rather than under developer pressure.

### Business Takeaway
**When a high-stakes agreement is being pushed to close faster than your information allows, impose your own moratorium.** The moratorium is a business tool: a formal, time-bounded period to gather data, evaluate options, and develop standards before committing. Businesses that allow external timeline pressure to override internal due diligence routinely sign agreements they later regret.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- When a vendor, customer, or partner presents a contract under deadline pressure, the deadline itself is a negotiating tactic. You are entitled to the time needed to evaluate the terms properly.
- Define your own information requirements before evaluating any major agreement: what do you need to know about pricing stability, service level commitments, exit terms, and performance guarantees? Gather that information before the deadline, not after.
- If a party is unwilling to extend a deadline for legitimate due diligence, that unwillingness is data about how they will behave as a partner.
- Build a formal evaluation checklist for major vendor and customer agreements that must be completed before approval. The checklist is your moratorium.

## 2. Not All Data Centers Are the Same: The Taxonomy Principle

The platform makes an explicit point from the May 18 council meeting: "data center" is a broad term. A facility built for one purpose will not require the same resources or create the same impacts as one built for another. The comparison table distinguishes five types across six dimensions: scale, water use, power draw, local jobs, and example use case.

Edge/colocation facilities draw 1–20 MW; AI/GPU clusters draw 200–1,000+ MW. The difference is not cosmetic — it is the difference between a manageable community asset and a facility that strains every public resource the city has.

### Business Takeaway
**Every category of vendor, customer, or partner contains a wide spectrum of actual profiles. Evaluate the specific instance, not the category.** A business that says "we work with distributors" without specifying which type, at what volume, and under what terms has not evaluated anything. The category is just a starting point.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- When evaluating a new vendor or customer relationship, build a profile of the specific entity across the dimensions that matter to your business: order volume, payment terms, support requirements, credit history, operational complexity.
- Do not approve a new customer or vendor based on category membership ("they're a national distributor") without evaluating the specific instance.
- Build a comparison table for your top vendor or customer segments that maps the dimensions your business cares about: revenue potential, service complexity, payment reliability, strategic alignment. Use it to make explicit comparisons, not intuitive ones.
- The most dangerous agreements are the ones where the category sounds good but the specific instance is high-risk. Category-level evaluation misses this every time.

## 3. Eight Findings of Fact: Evidence-Based Decision Making

The moratorium ordinance does not just say "we're concerned." It enumerates eight specific findings of fact, each sourced and stated with precision. These findings are the evidentiary basis for the legal action taken. They are also the framework for what the 180-day review must address.

This is the same discipline as the War Room's proof principle: declarations without evidence lose to evidence without declarations.

### Business Takeaway
**Every significant business decision should be backed by an enumerable, sourceable set of findings.** "We're concerned about this vendor" is not a decision basis. "We've identified five specific performance deficiencies with documented instances, dates, and business impact" is a decision basis. The findings format forces specificity and prevents vague concern from masquerading as analysis.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- When evaluating whether to change a vendor, terminate a customer relationship, or renegotiate a contract, document your findings before taking action: what specifically happened, when, what the business impact was, and what you need to see change.
- Present findings to internal stakeholders in the same format: numbered, specific, sourced. "We have three findings" is more credible and actionable than "we have some concerns."
- When a customer or vendor disputes a decision you've made, your documented findings are your defense. Undocumented decisions invite endless second-guessing.

## 4. What Was Promised vs. What Is Proposed: The Vision Gap

The 2019 Rock Row Master Plan envisioned a 2.5 million square foot mixed-use destination: retail, restaurants, a hotel, residential units, an amphitheater, and office space — drawing an estimated 5 million annual visitors. A data center was included as a permitted use, not as the primary use.

The community's concern is not with data centers in the abstract — it's with ensuring any proposal fits that vision, rather than replacing it with something that serves a corporation at the community's expense.

### Business Takeaway
**Every vendor or customer agreement should be evaluated against the original intent of the relationship, not just the current proposal.** When a distributor who was expected to drive retail volume proposes to shift to bulk/low-margin orders only, the proposal may be technically acceptable under the contract but misaligned with the original business case. The original intent is a legitimate evaluation standard.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- When a vendor or customer relationship is being renegotiated or expanded, explicitly compare the new proposal to the original intent of the relationship. Is this still the kind of business you intended to do together?
- Document the original business case for every major relationship: what were you expecting in terms of volume, margin, market access, or operational benefit? Use that documentation as an anchor in renegotiations.
- When a proposal would displace or replace something you intended to keep (a product line, a customer segment, a service capability), make that trade-off explicit. Don't let scope creep from one agreement replace an entire category of business activity.

## 5. The Risk Register: Naming What Can Go Wrong

The "If We Don't Get This Right" section enumerates eight specific, documented risks — each grounded in outcomes from other communities that moved too fast or negotiated too little:

- Utility bills rise for everyone
- Water drawn from a shared resource
- Noise that never stops
- Jobs that aren't local
- Tax revenue that doesn't last (depreciation reduces assessed value within 5–10 years)
- Heat islands near residential areas
- The original development vision quietly replaced
- No exit strategy — decommissioned facilities become the city's problem

Each risk is named, explained, and evidenced from real outcomes elsewhere. This is not fear-mongering — it is a risk register.

### Business Takeaway
**Every major agreement deserves a risk register before it is signed.** A risk register is not a list of reasons to say no. It is a list of specific, named outcomes that the agreement terms must address. If the agreement does not address the risk, the risk remains open. Open risks belong in the approval decision, not discovered after the contract is executed.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- Before finalizing any major vendor or customer agreement, produce a risk register: what are the specific ways this agreement could go wrong, and does the contract language address each one?
- The "no exit strategy" risk is particularly important: what happens when this relationship ends? Does the contract specify decommissioning obligations, data return, transition assistance, and termination terms?
- Depreciation and value decay apply to service agreements too: a pricing model that looks favorable today may be unfavorable in 3 years if market rates move. Build price review clauses.
- Share the risk register with everyone who will be affected by the agreement, not just the team that negotiated it. Hidden risks are the most expensive ones.

## 6. The Opportunity List: Negotiating Positions, Not Wishes

The "If We Do Get This Right" section is explicit: *"These aren't wishes — they're negotiating positions. Every item on the opportunity list has been achieved somewhere, by someone, who decided to ask."*

The eight opportunities include: high school internship pipelines, university research partnerships, waste heat recovery for community heating, decommissioned hardware for schools, a renewable energy anchor commitment, and a workforce training fund. Each is grounded in a real precedent from another community or city.

### Business Takeaway
**Community benefit terms — and vendor/customer benefit terms — must be asked for explicitly. They are never included by default.** A vendor who would readily agree to extended payment terms, dedicated support resources, or training access will not offer them unless asked. An agreement that does not include these terms because no one asked is not a neutral outcome — it is a missed negotiation.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- Before entering any major vendor negotiation, produce an opportunity list: what could you ask for that would make this relationship more valuable to your business beyond the core transaction?
- Common opportunities that are rarely asked for but often obtainable: priority order processing, dedicated account management, early access to new products, co-marketing support, training resources, extended warranty terms, volume rebate triggers.
- Ground each ask in a precedent: "We know other businesses in our segment have arrangements like this." Precedent-backed requests are harder to dismiss than requests that appear invented.
- Document what you asked for, what you received, and what was declined. This record is the baseline for the next negotiation.

## 7. The Comparison Table as a Decision Tool

The data center type comparison table maps five types across six impact dimensions. It is not presented as an argument for or against any type — it is presented as a framework for evaluation. The note underneath is explicit: *"Impact varies enormously by type. The 180-day period is an opportunity to define which types, if any, are appropriate — and under what conditions."*

This is the battlecard model applied to civic decision-making: a structured comparison that gives decision-makers a common reference point, not a conclusion.

### Business Takeaway
**A structured comparison table prevents decisions from being made on incomplete or selectively presented information.** When a vendor presents only the scenarios where they compare favorably, or a customer describes only the volume they expect (not the service complexity), the comparison is incomplete. A comprehensive comparison table — built by the evaluating party, not the pitching party — surfaces the full picture.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- For every major vendor or partner evaluation, build your own comparison table before reviewing the vendor's materials. Your table should include all relevant candidates and all dimensions that matter to your business.
- The vendor's pitch deck is not a comparison table — it is a marketing document. Use it as one data source among several.
- Include dimensions the vendor does not highlight: support response time, contract flexibility, exit terms, price escalation history, customer retention rate.
- A comparison table built by the buyer, evaluated consistently across all candidates, is the minimum standard for a defensible procurement decision.

## 8. The Countdown: Time-Bounded Decisions and Decision Quality

The platform includes a live countdown clock showing the remaining days, hours, minutes, and seconds of the 180-day moratorium window, with the label: *"180 days to get this right."*

This is not just a UI feature. It is a forcing function that makes the cost of inaction visible. A decision window that closes without a decision is itself a decision — the default is whatever happens when the moratorium expires without new standards.

### Business Takeaway
**Time-bounded decisions without visible deadlines default to inaction.** When a contract renewal, a vendor evaluation, or a pricing review has a deadline that is not surfaced and tracked, it will typically be addressed reactively — at the last moment, under pressure, with less information than was available earlier. The countdown clock is a data governance tool: it makes the cost of procrastination concrete.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- Every major agreement, contract, or vendor relationship should have an explicit renewal/review date tracked in your system — visible to the responsible owner with enough lead time to act deliberately.
- Set internal review triggers before external deadlines: a contract that renews on December 1 should have an internal review trigger on September 1 — not November 28.
- Use the moratorium model for internal decisions too: when a major system change, policy revision, or vendor transition is needed, set a formal decision window with a documented deadline and a defined deliverable.
- Track decision windows as open items until they are closed. A decision window that closes without a decision is a missed governance event.

## 9. Building for Replication: The Template Principle

The platform's footer is explicit: *"Built for Westbrook, Maine — and as a template for any community navigating this question."* The final item on the opportunity list names this explicitly: if Westbrook writes a community benefit agreement that works, it becomes the template for every town watching.

### Business Takeaway
**Every governance document, contract template, and evaluation framework you build for one situation should be designed to be reused.** A vendor evaluation process built from scratch every time is a governance process that never improves. A template built once, refined after each use, and shared across the organization compounds in quality over time.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- After every major vendor negotiation, contract review, or partnership evaluation, document what worked and what you would change. Update your templates accordingly.
- Publish your evaluation frameworks, contract templates, and risk registers internally. Teams that cannot access the institution's accumulated negotiating knowledge will re-learn the same lessons.
- When a negotiation produces a favorable outcome — a clause that protects your business, a term that proved valuable — add it to the template as a default ask for all future similar agreements.
- The goal is not just to win this negotiation. The goal is to build the organizational knowledge that wins all future negotiations of the same type.
