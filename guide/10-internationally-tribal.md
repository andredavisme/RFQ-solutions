# 10 — Internationally Tribal: AI as Creative Collaborator, Human Authorship, and the Direction vs. Execution Distinction

**Source repo:** [andredavisme/internationally-tribal](https://github.com/andredavisme/internationally-tribal)  
**Completed:** 2026-06-10  
**Theme:** A bold geometric SVG logo animation built as a demonstration of AI-assisted creative development. The repo includes the animated logo, a student activity guide, and a hub with sequential chapter activities. The central teaching is clear: *"AI is a hammer. A person with a hammer builds the house."* Every creative decision — the brand name, the aesthetic, the colors, the pirate-flag concept, the face transformation — came from a human. AI handled the code syntax. The vision was always human-led. These principles translate directly into how businesses should position AI-assisted work in their operations: the human brings the judgment; the tool brings the speed.

---

## 1. The Fear Is Real — and It Has a Specific Source

The activity guide opens by naming the fear directly: *"When most people first hear about AI, the conversation goes one of two ways: 'AI is going to replace artists, writers, and designers' or 'Just ask AI to do it for you.' Both of these frames have something in common — they remove you from the equation. And that's where the fear comes from."*

The fear is not irrational. It is a response to a specific, narrow way of using the tool — the outsourcing model, where the human pastes in a task, accepts the output, and calls it done. Used that way, AI does flatten creativity and does threaten authorship. The fear is a correct read of that specific use pattern.

The reframe is not "don't worry, AI won't replace you." It is: *"The question isn't 'will AI replace me?' — it's 'how do I use this thing to do more of what I actually want to do?'"*

### Business Takeaway
**The risk of AI in a business context is not that it will replace people — it is that it will be used in the outsourcing mode, which flattens judgment and produces generic outputs.** A quote generated by AI without a human reviewing it for accuracy, relationship context, and strategic fit is a generic quote. A customer response drafted by AI without a human verifying the facts and adjusting the tone is a generic response. The tool is only as good as the direction it receives.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- Identify every place in your operation where AI assistance is being used or could be used. For each, ask: is a human directing the output (reviewing, adjusting, approving), or is the output going directly to the customer or system without human judgment?
- The outsourcing mode — paste in, accept, send — is the mode that creates liability. A quote with a wrong price, a confirmation with a wrong date, a response with a wrong product spec — these are the errors that result from removing judgment from the loop.
- The collaboration mode — human directs, AI executes, human reviews — is the mode that amplifies productivity without sacrificing accuracy. Build this as your operational standard.
- Train your team on the distinction between using AI to speed up judgment-based work (collaboration mode) versus using AI to replace judgment-based work (outsourcing mode). The first is a productivity gain. The second is a quality and liability risk.

## 2. The Creative Brief Before the Prompt: Human-First Always

The activity guide requires students to answer five questions *before* writing a single prompt to AI:

1. What's the name or concept of your brand / project?
2. What 3 words describe the feeling or vibe you want?
3. What colors are you working with?
4. What letters, shapes, or symbols feel right to you?
5. What would it mean for your logo to "come alive"? What would it become?

The instruction is explicit: *"Write these down. These are your creative brief. Everything you say to AI should serve this brief."*

The rationale: *"AI cannot give you this. It can give you variations of things that already exist, mashed together in new ways. Your original idea — your brand, your aesthetic, your intention — that's the raw material AI needs to be useful."*

### Business Takeaway
**Every AI-assisted business output requires a human brief before the AI is engaged.** A proposal drafted with AI assistance that begins with "write a proposal for this customer" will produce a generic proposal. A proposal that begins with a brief — customer name, their stated priority, the differentiating factor we're leading with, the price point, the timeline commitment, the tone — will produce something that can be refined into a real deliverable. The brief is the raw material. Without it, AI has nothing to work with but averages.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- For every document type where AI assistance is used (quotes, proposals, customer communications, responses to RFQs, vendor briefs), define the required brief fields: what information must a human supply before the AI is engaged?
- Build brief templates for your most common AI-assisted work types. A quote brief template might include: customer name and history, product or service requested, key specs or requirements, price sensitivity signals, competitive context, desired tone, and any relationship factors. The brief takes 3 minutes. The output is transformed.
- The five-question model applies to any creative or strategic output: What is this for? What feeling or result do we want? What constraints apply? What elements are non-negotiable? What does success look like? These questions force human judgment into the process before the tool is invoked.
- Train staff to resist the "paste and go" instinct. The brief is not bureaucracy — it is the mechanism that converts AI from a generic output machine into a directed productivity tool.

## 3. You Direct, AI Codes: The Creative Director Model

The build activity establishes a clear division of labor: *"You direct, AI codes. You're the creative director. AI is the fast developer who can implement your instructions without needing lunch breaks."*

The iteration model is specific:
- Vague feedback is prohibited: "make it better" is not a direction
- Precise feedback is required: "The letter I needs to be further left and have pointed tips top and bottom"
- Each round: AI refines, you review, you redirect

The guide names what this skill is: *"This back-and-forth is the skill you're building — it's the same skill used in professional design, film direction, and product management."*

The face coordinates will need adjustment. This is framed as expected and normal: *"The face will likely need adjustment. This is normal — SVG coordinate space is abstract."* The willingness to iterate rather than accept the first output is itself a professional skill.

### Business Takeaway
**The creative director model is the correct mental model for every human-AI workflow in a business context.** The human is the director: they define the brief, review the output, identify what's wrong, provide specific correction, and approve the final product. The AI is the fast implementer: it executes instructions, generates options, and revises without ego. The director model keeps judgment in the human role and speed in the AI role.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- Train your team to be precise directors, not passive requesters. "Write a follow-up email to this customer" is a request. "Write a follow-up email to this customer confirming the revised delivery date of June 18, acknowledging the delay, and offering the 2% accommodation we discussed — tone should be direct and accountable, not apologetic" is a direction.
- Build a "review and redirect" step into every AI-assisted workflow. The first output is not the final output. The human reviews it, identifies what's wrong or missing, provides specific corrections, and reviews again. This cycle is the productive unit of AI-assisted work.
- Document common correction patterns for your team: what does AI typically get wrong in quotes for your product type? What tone adjustments are usually needed for your customer base? What details does it consistently omit? These patterns become your team's shared direction vocabulary.
- The iteration willingness — the professional acceptance that the first output is a draft, not a deliverable — is the most important habit to build. Businesses that treat the first AI output as final will produce consistently mediocre outputs. Businesses that treat it as a starting point will produce outputs that reflect actual judgment.

## 4. What AI Didn't Do: The Inventory of Human Authorship

The reflection section lists explicitly what AI did not do in the build:

- It didn't pick the brand name
- It didn't choose the colors or aesthetic
- It didn't decide the logo should turn into a face
- It didn't know when the face looked wrong
- It didn't know when to stop iterating

*"Every single one of those decisions was yours. AI was the hands. You were the mind."*

The framing of fear is revisited: *"The fear of AI is really the fear of losing authorship. You just proved that doesn't have to happen."*

This is the most operationally important section of the guide. It is an inventory of the irreplaceable human contributions to any AI-assisted output. None of these are technical skills. They are judgment skills: knowing what you want, recognizing when something is wrong, knowing when to stop.

### Business Takeaway
**The irreplaceable human contributions to any business output are not technical — they are judgment-based.** AI cannot know that a price is strategically wrong for this customer relationship. It cannot know that a delivery commitment is operationally unrealistic given current inventory. It cannot know that this vendor's quote has a quality history that makes the low price a risk. It cannot know when the communication has the right tone for this specific customer. These are judgment calls that require human context, and they are the most valuable thing a skilled business operator contributes.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- Build an inventory of the judgment calls that govern your most critical business outputs: quote approval, delivery commitment, vendor selection, escalation decisions, customer communication tone. These are the human authorship points. AI can draft; humans decide.
- Define the sign-off authority for each judgment call: who has the context and accountability to make the final call on a price, a commitment, a vendor selection, a customer response? These are your authorship points, and they should be explicitly owned, not implicitly assumed.
- Use the "what AI didn't do" frame as a training exercise: for any AI-assisted output, have your team enumerate what the human contributed. If the list is short, the process is in outsourcing mode and judgment is being abdicated.
- The fear of losing authorship is often a symptom of a process that hasn't defined where human judgment lives. Define it explicitly and the fear resolves: AI handles speed, humans handle judgment, and the output reflects both.

## 5. The 5-Phase Build: Direction at Every Stage

The build process is structured in five explicit phases, each with a defined human responsibility:

| Phase | Activity | Human's Role |
|-------|----------|--------------|
| 1 — Design | Define brand, aesthetic, colors, letterforms in SVG | Author the brief; reject what doesn't match the vision |
| 2 — Iterate | Review AI output, give specific corrections | Precise direction; no vague feedback |
| 3 — Animate | Define what the logo transforms into and how | Conceptual decision; AI executes |
| 4 — Tune | Adjust face to match the intended character | Aesthetic judgment; "keep going until it feels like your face" |
| 5 — Publish | Commit and deploy | Accountability for the final product |

Phase 4 carries a specific instruction: *"Keep going until the face feels like your face — not just whatever AI defaulted to."* The default is not good enough. The human's standard is the standard.

### Business Takeaway
**Every stage of a business deliverable has a human responsibility that cannot be delegated to the tool.** A quote that goes through five AI-assisted drafts but never has a human ask "does this reflect our actual relationship with this customer" has been refined without being directed. Refinement without direction produces polished generics. Direction with AI assistance produces accurate, context-specific deliverables.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- Map your most important deliverable types (RFQ responses, customer proposals, vendor briefs, service reports) to the 5-phase model: what is the brief phase, the iteration phase, the conceptual decision phase, the judgment-tuning phase, and the accountability/sign-off phase?
- Identify which phases are currently skipped. Most AI-assisted business workflows collapse to two phases: prompt and accept. The missing phases are where quality and accuracy live.
- The "keep going until it feels like yours" standard applies to any business communication: a customer response that feels like a template rather than a response written for this specific customer has not been tuned. The human's job is to close the gap between the draft and the standard.
- Build phase checkpoints into your AI-assisted workflows: a review gate after the first draft, a direction-adjustment step, and a final judgment review before the output is committed. These three gates cost minutes and prevent the errors that cost hours to correct.

## 6. Go Further: The Same Principle, Any Domain

The "Go Further" section lists extensions — all of which apply the same principle to new contexts:

- Apply it to any brand: swap shapes and colors
- Change the transformation: morph into an animal, a map, a symbol
- Add sound, interactivity, or a full brand page
- Generate logo variations using an AI image tool

The closing rule is the same in every case: *"You bring the idea, AI brings the execution speed. The creativity is always yours to protect."*

The phrase "yours to protect" is deliberate. It positions creativity and judgment as assets that require active stewardship — not passive byproducts of using the right tool.

### Business Takeaway
**Judgment, context, and relationship knowledge are business assets that require active stewardship.** They are not automatic. They erode when people stop exercising them — when AI is allowed to make the calls that humans should be making, when process replaces judgment, when speed is optimized at the expense of accuracy. The businesses that protect these assets will differentiate. The businesses that outsource them will commoditize.

**Applied to RFQ/service workflows:**
- Identify the judgment assets that differentiate your business from a generic competitor: knowledge of specific customer preferences, relationships with key vendor contacts, understanding of which products perform well in which conditions, history with a customer's decision-making process. These are your assets. AI cannot replicate them; it can only be directed by them.
- Build processes that capture and preserve judgment: when a skilled employee makes a non-obvious call ("we quoted this customer below standard margin because of the volume commitment they're signaling"), document the reasoning. That reasoning is institutional knowledge. When the employee leaves, the documented reasoning stays.
- Apply the "yours to protect" standard to your customer data, pricing knowledge, vendor relationships, and operational know-how. These are not just inputs to AI tools — they are competitive assets that must be stewarded, not just used.
- The Go Further principle: the same discipline of human-directed AI collaboration applies across every function — customer communication, vendor negotiation, operations management, financial reporting. In every domain, the human brings the vision and the judgment. The tool brings the speed.
