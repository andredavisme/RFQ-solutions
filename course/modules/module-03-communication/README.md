# Module 03: Communication and Commitment Discipline

## Purpose

Replace vague language with verifiable commitments.

The most expensive failures in business communication are not lies — they are statements that sound definitive but aren't. "We'll get back to you shortly." "We typically deliver within the week." "That should be fine." These phrases feel like communication. They aren't. They are *declarations without proof*, and they create the conditions for misaligned expectations, escalations, and eroded trust.

This module builds three interlocking disciplines: **voice** (how you write), **commitment structure** (what you promise and how), and **proof anchoring** (how you verify claims before making them).

## Core Concepts

### Declarations vs. Proof

A declaration is an assertion: "We deliver fast." "Our team is responsive." "Quality is our priority."

A proof is a verifiable claim: "98% of orders shipped within 24 hours of confirmation in the last 90 days." "Average first-response time: 4 business hours."

Declarations feel like communication but carry no information. They cannot be evaluated, tracked, or held to account. Proof-based communication is falsifiable — which is exactly what makes it trustworthy.

The discipline: before making any claim about capability, speed, or quality, ask: *what data would make this claim verifiable?* If no data exists, either produce it or don't make the claim.

### Voice Discipline

Voice is the tone and structure of written communication. Poor voice has three failure modes:

1. **Passive voice** — obscures accountability ("The shipment was delayed" vs. "Our carrier delayed the shipment on Tuesday")
2. **Vague quantifiers** — creates ambiguity ("shortly," "ASAP," "soon," "typically")
3. **Performative formality** — adds length without meaning ("Please be advised that we have taken note of your inquiry and will endeavor to respond at our earliest convenience")

Voice discipline means: active voice, specific values, and direct structure. The message should state what happened, what will happen, when, and by whom — in that order when applicable.

### Specific Commitments

A commitment is specific when:
- It has a **date or condition** attached (not "soon" — "by Friday, June 13" or "within 24 hours of receiving the PO")
- It has a **named owner** (not "we'll look into it" — "I will confirm with our warehouse team by 3pm today")
- It is **observable** — the recipient can verify whether the commitment was met

Vague commitments are not kindness — they are liability deferred. A specific commitment that is missed can be acknowledged and recovered from. A vague commitment that is missed is experienced as a broken promise.

### The Objection as Data

Customer objections are not obstacles — they are signals. An objection contains three data points:
1. The **surface concern** (what was said)
2. The **underlying need** (what is actually at stake)
3. The **proof gap** (what evidence would resolve it)

A structured objection response uses proof anchors: it names the underlying need, provides specific evidence that addresses it, and offers a verifiable next step. This is communication as information exchange, not performance.

## Work Lab

Learners receive a sample customer update email thread — three responses, each containing at least one declaration, one vague time reference, and one passive construction.

The task:
1. Highlight every declaration, vague quantifier, and passive construction
2. Rewrite the full thread using voice discipline rules
3. Add a specific commitment to each response (date + owner + observable outcome)
4. Compare the rewritten thread against a peer's version before the model reveal
