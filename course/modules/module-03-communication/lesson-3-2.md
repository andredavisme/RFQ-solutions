# Lesson 3.2 — Voice Discipline

**Module:** 03 — Communication and Commitment Discipline
**Concept:** Rewrite passive, vague, and performative messages into direct, specific, human ones

---

## Context

Lesson 3.1 addressed the content problem: declarations masquerading as information. This lesson addresses the structure problem: how vague, passive, and performatively formal writing makes even accurate information harder to act on.

Voice discipline is not a style preference. It is a functional requirement. A message that obscures accountability, delays the point, or requires three readings to parse is a message that creates work for the reader and risk for the sender.

---

## Concept: The Three Voice Failures

### Failure 1: Passive Voice

Passive voice removes the actor from the sentence. When no actor is named, no one is accountable.

| Passive | Active + Accountable |
|---------|----------------------|
| "The shipment was delayed." | "Our carrier delayed shipment on Tuesday due to a routing error." |
| "Your request has been received." | "I received your request at 9:14am and assigned it to our fulfillment team." |
| "It was determined that the part is no longer available." | "I confirmed with our warehouse this morning: PRV-220B is out of stock through July." |
| "Mistakes were made in the processing of your order." | "We entered the wrong quantity on your order. I've corrected it and will send an updated confirmation within the hour." |

Active voice names who did what. This is not just grammatically cleaner — it is functionally different. The customer now knows who to follow up with, what was done, and when.

### Failure 2: Vague Quantifiers

Vague time and quantity words create ambiguity that customers fill in with their best-case assumptions — which are rarely aligned with your operational reality.

| Vague | Specific |
|-------|----------|
| "shortly" | "by 5pm today" / "within 2 business hours" |
| "soon" | "by end of week" / "by Thursday, June 13" |
| "ASAP" | "I'm working on this now — you'll have it by noon" |
| "a few days" | "3 business days — by Wednesday, June 15" |
| "typically" | "in 96% of cases" / "based on last quarter's data" |
| "usually" | same as above — replace with a measured rate or a specific date |

If you cannot provide a specific date or rate, say so explicitly and explain why: "I can't confirm the ship date until I hear back from the vendor. I'll follow up by 3pm today with either a confirmed date or an explanation of the hold."

This is more informative and more trustworthy than any vague quantifier.

### Failure 3: Performative Formality

Performatively formal language adds length and tone without adding information. It signals effort and care while delivering neither.

| Performative | Direct |
|--------------|--------|
| "Please be advised that we have taken careful note of your inquiry and will endeavor to respond at our earliest convenience." | "I received your inquiry and will respond by tomorrow at noon." |
| "We sincerely apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused and want to assure you that we take your satisfaction very seriously." | "I'm sorry this happened. Here's what I'm doing to fix it and when you can expect resolution." |
| "Thank you for bringing this important matter to our attention." | [Delete. Get to the point.] |
| "We are committed to providing you with the highest level of service possible." | [Delete. Show it; don't say it.] |

Performative formality is often well-intentioned — it signals respect and care. But in a time-sensitive business communication, it is experienced as delay. The customer wants information, not ceremony.

### The Four-Element Response Structure

Every substantive customer communication should contain, in order:

1. **What happened** (or what the situation is) — one sentence, active voice
2. **What you are doing about it** — one sentence, named owner, active verb
3. **When the customer will hear back** — specific date/time or condition
4. **What the customer should do in the meantime** (if anything) — only include if relevant

This structure is not a template to copy — it is a completeness check. Before sending any customer message, verify it contains all four elements that apply to the situation.

## Work Lab: Rewrite Drill

You are given a five-email thread between a sales rep and a customer. The thread contains:
- 6 instances of passive voice
- 8 instances of vague quantifiers ("shortly," "soon," "ASAP," etc.)
- 4 instances of performative formality

Your task:
1. Mark every instance with its failure type (P = passive, V = vague, F = formal)
2. Rewrite the full thread using the four-element response structure
3. Count the word reduction between the original and rewritten versions
4. Identify the one sentence in your rewrite that carries the most information per word

## Reflection

1. **Past:** What phrase do you most frequently use in customer communications that functions as a vague quantifier or performative formality? When did you start using it?
2. **Present:** Read the last three customer-facing messages you sent. Mark every passive construction, vague quantifier, and performative phrase. What percentage of the words were carrying information?
3. **Future:** Write a personal voice rule — one sentence that describes how you will structure customer status updates going forward.

## Key Principle

> Every word in a customer message is spending the customer's time. Passive voice spends it on obscurity. Vague quantifiers spend it on ambiguity. Performative formality spends it on ceremony. Voice discipline returns it.
